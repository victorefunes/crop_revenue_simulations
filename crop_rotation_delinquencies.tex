\begin{abstract}
Operating credit in row-crop agriculture is path-dependent: unpaid loan balances roll forward and compound, so a single bad year can initiate a multi-year delinquency spiral. Yet credit underwriting and crop insurance pricing treat farm revenue as an aggregate stream, ignoring how crop rotation history shapes both yield distributions and the cash-flow process that determines whether harvest revenue retires the operating loan. This paper develops a simulation framework that couples field-level, rotation-conditioned yield distributions, estimated from a fifteen-year Illinois corn panel, with an explicit operating-credit model that tracks origination, accrual, repayment, and balance rollover. Delinquency risk follows a sawtooth pattern: soybean-harvest years periodically retire accumulated rollover debt, reducing the six-year cumulative delinquency probability by 13 to 27 percentage points relative to continuous corn, depending on region and productivity zone. A Shapley decomposition assigns roughly 84 to 100 percent of delinquency risk to price uncertainty. Separately, the natural price-yield hedge, measured as the difference between correlated and independent price-yield draws, is small and largely redundant where rotation has already reset the debt balance. Rotation reduces the price-driven risk not by lowering price uncertainty but by restructuring the cost-and-debt path the price shocks propagate through. A comparison between independent and correlated prices and yields confirms that the rotation channel operates via the timing of debt retirement, rather than aggregate price effects. Rotation history is an observable risk factor that current credit and insurance instruments systematically ignore.
\end{abstract}
 
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Crop rotation; Operating credit; Farm delinquency; Price-yield dependence; Revenue risk

\section{\label{sec:introduction}Introduction}
 
Operating credit is the financial lifeline of row-crop farms in the United States. Farmers borrow against the spring planting decision, accrue interest through the growing season, and retire the balance at harvest from realized revenue. When harvest revenue falls short of the obligation, the unpaid balance rolls forward to the next year's operating note, compounds at a contractually fixed rate, and enlarges the principal financed in the next planting cycle. A bad year is not a single shock but the first link in a chain: what the borrower carries into year $t+1$ is jointly determined by the realized revenue draw and the agronomic state of the field, both of which depend on what was planted in years $t-5$ through $t$.
 
This path dependence is invisible to the credit-risk models that lenders, insurers, and regulators currently use. Operating-credit underwriting treats farm revenue as an aggregate stream, summarized by expected value and variance, and predicts default using balance-sheet ratios calibrated to one-year loss probabilities \citep{Featherstone2006}. Crop insurance, the dominant federal risk-management instrument, prices revenue protection against single-year shortfalls from a county-level Actual Production History (APH) baseline \citep{Goodwin2004}; the rolling-five-year APH window adjusts the baseline mechanically but does not model how rotation history shapes the conditional yield distribution from which next year's revenue is drawn. Agronomy has long documented that yields depend on the sequence of crops in prior years: the corn-following-corn yield penalty, the soybean nitrogen credit, and the productivity gains from diverse rotations are textbook material \citep{Bullock1992, Seifert2017}, but the agronomic literature stops at agronomy. Yield is the endpoint, not a state variable that feeds a financial process. Despite these documented yield benefits, rotation diversity in the U.S.\ Midwest has declined over the past century, with simplification concentrated on the most productive land and reinforced by proximity to corn-demanding policy infrastructure, such as biofuel plants and grain elevators \citep{socolar2021}. The financial case for rotation, if it exists, has not yet been made in terms that the parties pricing farm credit risk can act on.
 
We develop a simulation framework that couples field-level, rotation-conditioned yield distributions, estimated from a fifteen-year panel covering every corn field in Illinois, with an explicit operating-credit cash-flow model that tracks intra-year debt origination, nine-month interest accrual, harvest-time repayment, and balance rollover under insufficient revenue. The framework produces a multi-year delinquency probability (the probability that a farm incurs at least one operating-loan default over a six-year horizon), differentiated by the producer's rotation plan and conditional on the six-year history of crops planted in the field. The simulation embeds a Gaussian representation of the negative correlation between yields and market prices, calibrated from county-level price--yield correlations and applied only to the systematic component of yield, so the natural hedge enters at the appropriate field-level intensity rather than the aggregate intensity \citep{Finger2012, Ramsey2019}.
 
The simulation produces three findings that, taken together, reframe how rotation choice should be understood in financial terms.
 
\textbf{First, the time path of delinquency risk is not a smooth curve; it is a sawtooth.} Under continuous corn, the annual probability of operating-loan default is roughly flat, drifting up slightly from year 1 to year 6 in every cell, with no year-to-year decline; its level depends on productivity, at about 5--7 percent per year in the high-productivity zones, 8--12 percent in the medium zones, and 12--27 percent in the low zones. Under either rotation plan, the per-year probability drops to near zero in soybean-harvest years and rebuilds during corn years. The mechanism is not statistical noise: a soybean harvest retires the accumulated rollover balance because soy is planted at a lower spring loan and benefits from a more favorable price-cost ratio in the years studied. Rotation protects against multi-year ruin not by uniformly reducing risk but by \textit{periodically arresting} the debt-rollover process that converts a bad year into a multi-year default. The cumulative six-year delinquency probability falls from 32.1 percent under continuous corn in central Illinois's high-productivity zone to 17.5 percent under strict alternation, and from 54.8 to 32.2 percent in the central low-productivity zone; across the nine cells the reduction is 13 to 27 percentage points, concentrated in the soybean years.
 
\textbf{Second, price risk dominates revenue variance, and the negative price-yield correlation provides only a modest natural hedge at the field level.} A variance decomposition of gross revenue assigns 82--96 percent of variation to price and 4--18 percent to yield across regions, zones, and rotation plans, with the price share highest in the high-productivity zones. A Shapley attribution of delinquency risk to its two stochastic inputs is similarly lopsided: price uncertainty explains roughly 84 to 100 percent of the probability of delinquency, with yield uncertainty accounting for the residual. The negative price-yield correlation does reduce delinquency, but the reduction is small (at most 6.4 percentage points in any cell) and shrinks as rotation does more of the work. The natural hedge and rotation are partial substitutes in the cash-flow space: continuous corn gains most from the hedge because it never retires its rollover balance, while rotation plans already drive year-end debt to near zero in soybean years, so the hedge has less additional buffering to perform.
 
\textbf{Third, the rotation effect materializes quickly: the survival gap opens within the first corn-soy cycle.} Survival curves separate across rotation plans almost immediately. By year 2, the gap between continuous corn and strict alternation is already 5.0--16.1 percentage points across the nine region $\times$ zone cells, and it reaches 12.8--26.6 points by year 6. The step structure of the curves, with drops concentrated in corn years and flat segments in soybean years, mirrors the sawtooth in the per-year hazard and confirms that early delinquency events do most of the lasting damage: once a field enters the delinquency state under continuous corn, it tends not to leave it.
 
The empirical contribution sits among this set of findings. The methodological contribution is the framework that produces them: a yield generator that conditions nonparametrically on the full six-year rotation state, a credit module that preserves the within-year sequence of origination, accrual, repayment, and rollover (rather than collapsing the year into a single net cash flow), and a price-yield copula that distinguishes field-level from aggregate correlation. None of these components is individually new; they draw on \citet{Burt1963} and \citet{Livingston2015} for the dynamic-state structure, on \citet{Featherstone2006, Ifft2015} for the operating-credit calibration, and on \citet{Ramsey2019} for the copula specification; but their combination produces a previously unavailable object: a multi-year, rotation-conditional default probability that can be compared across plans, regions, and productivity zones.
 
The findings carry direct implications for credit underwriting and insurance pricing. For lenders, rotation history is an observable, verifiable risk factor: the simulation shows that two farms with identical balance sheets but different six-year rotation states face six-year delinquency probabilities that differ by a factor of 1.6 to 1.8 across cells, or by 13 to 27 percentage points. For insurers, the sawtooth pattern in per-year risk implies that revenue-protection products calibrated to single-year loss ratios will systematically misprice the multi-year exposure of corn-heavy producers, who carry their year-1 shocks forward in ways that the APH baseline does not capture. We take up these implications in Section~\ref{sec:implications}.

Section~\ref{sec:theory} sets out the farmer's decision problem and establishes two propositions linking rotation choice to expected utility through the operating-credit recursion. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the field-level Illinois panel and calibrates the yield, price, and cost distributions. Section~\ref{sec:simulation} develops the simulation model. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the main results. Section~\ref{sec:robustness} reports the variance and Shapley decompositions and survival-curve analysis. Section~\ref{sec:implications} discusses implications for lenders and insurers. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.
 
\section{\label{sec:framing}Framing and related literature}
 
The paper sits at the intersection of three literatures that have so far developed in parallel.
 
The \textbf{rotation-yield literature} establishes the agronomic facts. \citet{Bullock1992} surveys the carry-over effects from prior cropping: corn-after-corn yield penalties, soybean nitrogen credits in the corn-after-soy direction, and rotation-complexity gains from longer cycles. \citet{Seifert2017} estimate the corn-after-corn penalty empirically using satellite-derived yields, placing it at 4.3 percent across hundreds of thousands of Midwestern fields. More recent work pushes this strand beyond mean yields. \citet{Bowles2020} synthesize 347 site-years across 11 long-term experiments in North America and show that diversified rotations reduce corn yield losses by 14--89 percent in drought years (identified using county-level crop-insurance indemnity data) while also lowering the probability of yields below the 10th percentile at 8 of 11 sites. \citet{Smith2023} extend this to 32 long-term European and North American experiments and document that yield benefits from diversification increase over time, driven particularly by crop functional richness under low external nitrogen input. These papers establish that rotation provides not just a mean-yield bump but a reduction in yield-tail risk that compounds over the planning horizon. Yet the empirical adoption pattern runs counter to this: \citet{socolar2021}, using a field-level rotational complexity index applied to 1.5 million Midwestern fields, documents a century-long simplification trend that is strongest on the most productive land and reinforced by proximity to corn-demanding policy infrastructure. The puzzle is not whether rotation has benefits (the agronomic literature settles that), but why those documented benefits are insufficient to halt simplification, and whether a financial benefit visible to lenders and insurers could change the calculus.
 
This strand documents a risk-reduction channel but stops at the yield distribution. Whether yield-tail compression translates into financial-tail compression depends on the cash-flow process the farm operates under, which the rotation-yield papers do not address: a known agronomic risk-reduction channel mapped through an explicit credit-flow process to a financial-survival statement that lenders and insurers can act on.
 
The \textbf{dynamic crop-choice literature} begins with \citet{Burt1963}, who formulated the wheat-fallow problem with soil moisture as a state variable and solved it using stochastic dynamic programming, and continues with \citet{Livingston2015} as the closest structural predecessor. They solve the optimal sequential planting problem for corn and soy under price uncertainty, treating the prior crop as a state variable; the financial process is implicit. The most recent extension is \citet{Chen2025}, which solves a stochastic dynamic program for optimal cover-crop adoption in a 35-year Tennessee cotton experiment, incorporating a cumulative soil-fertility state variable and irreversibility from sunk machinery investments. We hold the rotation policy fixed and trace its consequences through an explicit operating-credit cash-flow process to a financial-survival outcome rather than solving for the optimal policy.
 
The \textbf{farm credit risk literature} has developed along three lines that together supply the financial-distress measurement and operating-credit conventions this paper draws on, but none condition on rotation history. \citet{Featherstone2006} calibrate probability-of-default and loss-given-default models on the Seventh Farm Credit District portfolio. \citet{IfftKuethe2013} document the credit-insurance interaction at the farm level: federal crop insurance participation is associated with higher leverage \citep{Ifft2015} and a higher probability of credit default \citep{IfftKuethe2013}. The sign of this relationship is not settled. \citet{LeeEtAl2024}, using a 1994--2015 county-level panel of Midwestern corn production, find the opposite: counties with higher crop insurance participation rates carry statistically \textit{lower} agricultural loan delinquency rates. The two results are reconcilable if insurance raises leverage at the farm level while stabilizing revenue enough to lower realized delinquency in the aggregate, but they bracket rather than corroborate one another. \citet{Kim2020} extend the financial-distress object beyond default to exit, estimating how crop insurance affects farm disinvestment and survival. Across all three lines, the state space includes the balance sheet, the income statement, and the loan terms; it does not include rotation history. The simulation shows that two farms with identical financial statements but different agronomic trajectories face six-year delinquency probabilities that differ by 13 to 27 percentage points, depending on region and productivity zone: rotation state is a missing variable in current underwriting practice.
 
\section{\label{sec:theory}Theoretical Framework}
 
Before developing the simulation, it is useful to establish the farmer's decision problem that motivates why rotation choice has financial content. The model is deliberately spare: it shows that a risk-averse farmer's optimal rotation plan depends on the operating-credit cash-flow process, not just expected revenue, and that the simulation in Section~\ref{sec:simulation} evaluates the farmer's objective under uncertainty.
 
\subsection{\label{subsec:setup}Setup}
 
Consider a farmer who chooses a rotation plan $r$ at the start of a $T$-period horizon. A plan is a deterministic sequence of crop choices $r = (c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_T)$, where $c_t \in \{\text{corn}, \text{soy}\}$. The farmer commits to the plan ex ante, an assumption that abstracts from adjusting the plan within the horizon and from any cost of switching crops. Let $\mathcal{R}$ denote the finite set of feasible plans; the three plans analyzed here (CCCCCC, CSCSCS, CCSCCS) are elements of $\mathcal{R}$.
 
\subsection{\label{subsec:constraint}Operating-credit constraint}
 
In each year $t$, the farmer must finance spring planting. Let $B_t(r)$ denote the debt carried out of year $t$ under plan $r$, equivalently the balance entering year $t+1$, with $B_0 = 0$. The within-year credit recursion is:
\begin{equation}
B_t(r) = \max\!\Bigl(0,\;\bigl[C_t(r) + B_{t-1}(r)\bigr](1+i/12)^{9}
- P_t\,Y_t(r, s_t)\Bigr),
\end{equation}
where $C_t(r)$ is the crop-specific operating cost, $i$ is the operating-loan rate, $P_t$ is the realized harvest price, and $Y_t(r, s_t)$ is drawn from the rotation-conditioned yield distribution $F_{Y \mid s_t, c, z}$ estimated in Section~\ref{subsec:generator}. A delinquency event in year $t$ is $\mathbb{1}\{B_t(r) > 0\}$.
 
\subsection{\label{subsec:farmer_problem}Farmer's problem}
 
The farmer's preferences over wealth are represented by a von Neumann-Morgenstern utility function $u(\cdot)$ with $u' > 0$ and $u'' < 0$. Define cumulative net revenue over the horizon as:
\begin{equation}
W_T(r) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \delta^{t-1}\bigl[P_t\,Y_t(r, s_t) - C_t(r) - \bigl[(1+i/12)^{9}-1\bigr] L_t(r)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\delta$ is a discount factor and $[(1+i/12)^{9}-1]L_t(r)$ is the nine-month interest cost of the spring operating loan (monthly compounding), matching the accrual convention of the simulation recursion in Section~\ref{subsec:recursion}. The farmer chooses the rotation plan to maximize expected utility of terminal wealth net of the shadow cost of delinquency:
\begin{equation}
r^{*} = \arg\max_{r \in \mathcal{R}}\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[u\!\bigl(W_T(r)\bigr)\;-\;\lambda \sum_{t=1}^{T} \delta^{t-1}\,\mathbb{1}\{B_t(r)>0\}\right]
\end{equation}
where $\lambda \geq 0$ is the shadow cost of a delinquency event, capturing reputational, legal, and credit-access penalties beyond the direct financial loss.
 
Because $\mathcal{R}$ is finite and discrete, the problem does not admit classical first-order conditions. The solution is a comparison of the objective across plans, precisely what the simulation in Section~\ref{sec:simulation} computes.
 
\subsection{\label{subsec:propositions}Propositions}
 
\subsubsection{\label{subsubsec:proposition_1}Lower-tail stochastic dominance}
 
Full first-order stochastic dominance of $F_{\text{CS}}$ over $F_{\text{CC}}$ would require $F_{\text{CS}}(y) \leq F_{\text{CC}}(y)$ for all $y$: too strong and almost certainly false at the upper tail. Two weaker conditions do the work. The first concerns delinquency and is the condition of \textbf{lower-tail dominance}: there exists a threshold $\bar{y}(c,z)$ such that:
\begin{equation}
F_{Y \mid s^{\text{CS}}, c, z}(y)\;\leq\;F_{Y \mid s^{\text{CC}}, c, z}(y)\quad \forall\; y \leq \bar{y}(c,z). \tag{LTD}
\end{equation}
The second concerns utility: rotation yields must second-order stochastically dominate continuous-corn yields. Given a single crossing of the two CDFs, with $F_{\text{CS}}$ below $F_{\text{CC}}$ before the crossing and above it afterwards, this follows from the condition that rotation does not lower mean yield, $\mathbb{E}[Y_{\text{CS}}] \geq \mathbb{E}[Y_{\text{CC}}]$ \citep{Hanoch1969, Rothschild1970}. The mean condition is what \citet{Bullock1992} and \citet{Seifert2017} document.

\textbf{Agronomic mechanism}: The yield penalty from continuous corn is concentrated in stress years, not symmetric around the mean. Two mechanisms produce this asymmetry. First, rootworm pressure accumulates under continuous corn, causing root lodging precisely in drought years when plants are already water-stressed and least able to compensate for root damage. Second, the soil pathogen and pest cycle that the soybean break interrupts operates nonlinearly: at high pest densities (which build up over multiple consecutive corn years) losses are severe and correlated with other stressors. The rotation benefit is largest exactly when conditions are worst.
 
\textbf{Empirical support in the Illinois panel}: The nonparametric yield distributions estimated in Section~\ref{subsec:generator} allow direct verification of (LTD) within each $(c,z)$ cell. Comparing corn after soybeans in a strict alternation with continuous corn, the dominance condition holds at the 10th percentile in 7 of the 9 cells and at the 25th percentile in 7 of 9; the exceptions are the northern-high and northern-medium cells, where the rotation CDF evaluated at the continuous-corn 10th percentile is 10.1 and 10.9 percent and at the 25th percentile is 26.0 and 27.9 percent. At the delinquency threshold $\bar{y}$ the rotation CDF is no higher than the continuous-corn CDF in 7 cells, and in the other two (northern-high and southern-high) both CDFs are below 0.02 percent. Below $\bar{y}$ the largest excess of $F_{\text{CS}}$ over $F_{\text{CC}}$ is 0.15 percentage points, within Monte Carlo error. The two distributions are close: the simulated mean corn yield after soybeans differs from continuous corn by between $-0.9$ and $+0.9$ bu/ac, so the mean-yield condition $\mathbb{E}[Y_{\text{CS}}] \geq \mathbb{E}[Y_{\text{CC}}]$ holds in only four of the nine cells (it fails by less than 1 bu/ac in the other five). The CDFs do not cross significantly in northern-high and northern-medium, cross once in six cells, and cross three times in southern-medium. In short, the data support (LTD) only approximately in this panel, and the proposition's conditions are not satisfied uniformly.\footnote{The check uses 50,000 draws per state from the estimated generators. The southern continuous-corn states rest on few observations (734 to 2,603 field-years), so the southern comparisons are the least precise.} Figure~\ref{fig:ltd_cdf_comparison} plots the two CDFs for all nine region $\times$ zone cells, together with the cell-specific delinquency threshold $\bar{y}$.

\begin{theorem}[Lower-tail dominance and the rotation utility premium]
\label{prop:ltd}

Consider the first year of the horizon, with $B_0 = 0$ and the harvest price fixed at its median $\bar{P}$. Let $\bar{y}(c,z) = C_1(1+i/12)^{9} / \bar{P}$, the yield at which revenue exactly covers the accrued operating cost. Suppose the yield CDFs cross at most once, from below, with $\mathbb{E}[Y_{\text{CS}}] \geq \mathbb{E}[Y_{\text{CC}}]$, and suppose (LTD) holds at $\bar{y}(c,z)$. Then for any $\lambda \geq 0$ and any increasing concave $u$,
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}\left[u\bigl(W_1(\text{CS})\bigr) - \lambda\,\mathbb{1}\{B_1(\text{CS})>0\}\right]\,\geq\,\mathbb{E}\left[u\bigl(W_1(\text{CC})\bigr) - \lambda\,\mathbb{1}\{B_1(\text{CC})>0\}\right].
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
 
\begin{remark}
The inequality is strict when $\lambda > 0$ and $F_{\text{CS}}(\bar{y}) < F_{\text{CC}}(\bar{y})$. The multi-period comparison is carried by Proposition~\ref{prop:credit_path}.
\end{remark}
 
\begin{proof}
With $B_0 = 0$ and $P = \bar{P}$, wealth $W_1(r) = \bar{P}Y - C_1(1+i/12)^{9}$ is increasing and affine in $Y$, and $B_1(r) > 0$ exactly when $Y < \bar{y}$. The single crossing and the mean condition imply $\int_{-\infty}^{y}[F_{\text{CC}}(x) - F_{\text{CS}}(x)]\,dx \geq 0$ for every $y$: the integrand is nonnegative up to the crossing point, and after it the running integral can only fall toward its limit $\mathbb{E}[Y_{\text{CS}}] - \mathbb{E}[Y_{\text{CC}}] \geq 0$. This is second-order stochastic dominance of $Y_{\text{CS}}$ over $Y_{\text{CC}}$, so $\mathbb{E}[u(W_1(\text{CS}))] \geq \mathbb{E}[u(W_1(\text{CC}))]$ for every increasing concave $u$. The delinquency terms are $\lambda\Pr[Y < \bar{y}]$ under each plan, and by (LTD) this probability is weakly smaller under CS, so the $\lambda$ penalty is weakly smaller under rotation. Both components are weakly larger under CS.
\end{proof}
 
The threshold $\bar{y}(c,z)$ is the delinquency threshold at the median price, not an arbitrary quantile. At the baseline costs it is 133 to 140 bu/ac across cells (139.6 in central-high, computed at the median cost and median price, $\bar{y} = C(1+0.09/12)^{9}/\bar{P}$), far below the mean corn yield, so both CDFs are close to zero there (Figure~\ref{fig:ltd_cdf_comparison}). The yield channel formalized here therefore contributes little to delinquency in our calibration, which is the same fact as the Shapley result in Section~\ref{subsec:shapley} that price uncertainty accounts for nearly all delinquency risk. The sample CDFs are close to each other and do not show first-order dominance of rotation in this panel; the proposition needs only the weaker conditions above, which the panel supports approximately rather than uniformly. The result holds for any $\lambda \geq 0$; delinquency costs need only be nonnegative, and strictness requires them to be positive.
 
\subsubsection{\label{subsubsec:proposition_2}Rotation lowers carried debt in every period}
 
Proposition~\ref{prop:ltd} establishes the first-year result. Proposition~\ref{prop:credit_path} carries the comparison to every later year through the operating-credit recursion, which is where the debt reset operates.

\begin{theorem}[Rotation lowers carried debt in every period]
\label{prop:credit_path}

Write the year-$t$ net margin as $m_t(r) = R_t(r) - (1+i/12)^{9}C_t(r)$, so the recursion is $B_t(r) = \max\{0,\; (1+i/12)^{9}B_{t-1}(r) - m_t(r)\}$ with $B_0 = 0$ and the same initial history under both plans. Suppose margins are independent across years and that, in every year $t$, $m_t(\text{CS})$ first-order stochastically dominates $m_t(\text{CC})$. Then $B_t(\text{CS})$ is stochastically smaller than $B_t(\text{CC})$ for every $t$, so $\Pr[B_t(\text{CS}) > 0] \leq \Pr[B_t(\text{CC}) > 0]$ in every year, and the six-year delinquency probability is no larger under rotation:
\begin{equation}
\Pr\Bigl[\max_{t \leq T} B_t(\text{CS}) > 0\Bigr]\;\leq\;\Pr\Bigl[\max_{t \leq T} B_t(\text{CC}) > 0\Bigr].
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
 
\begin{proof}
Couple the two plans year by year through the quantile transform so that $m_t(\text{CS}) \geq m_t(\text{CC})$ almost surely in every year, with independence across years preserved. The map $b \mapsto \max\{0,\; (1+i/12)^{9}b - m\}$ is increasing in $b$ and decreasing in $m$. Starting from $B_0 = 0$ under both plans, induction gives $B_t(\text{CS}) \leq B_t(\text{CC})$ almost surely for every $t$. Hence $\{B_t(\text{CS}) > 0\} \subseteq \{B_t(\text{CC}) > 0\}$ for each $t$, and the union over $t \leq T$ is nested in the same way.
\end{proof}
 
The margin condition is an assumption about the data-generating process, not a consequence of the recursion. In a plan's soybean years it says the soybean margin beats the corn margin in distribution, which the lower soybean cost helps deliver, and in its corn years it says corn after soybeans does at least as well as corn after corn. The simulation allows both to be checked cell by cell. The argument is the debt-reset mechanism in general form: a plan that raises the margin in any year lowers carried debt in that year and, through the recursion, in every later year.

Taken together, the propositions imply that rotation lowers delinquency probability in every period and, in the first year, raises expected utility. A farmer who weights delinquency more heavily (a higher effective $\lambda$: lower off-farm income, thinner equity buffers, or weaker lender relationships) gains more from this, so rotation adoption should be higher among such farmers, consistent with the adoption patterns in \citet{Ifft2015}.
 
\subsection{\label{relation_sim}Relation to the simulation}
 
The simulation evaluates the probability of delinquency across three fixed rotation plans, treating $\lambda$ and $u$ as implicit. It does not solve for $r^*$ or estimate structural preference parameters. By Proposition~\ref{prop:ltd}, under the stated conditions rotation raises first-year expected utility net of the delinquency penalty for any $\lambda \geq 0$ and any increasing concave $u$; by Proposition~\ref{prop:credit_path}, rotation lowers carried debt and the delinquency probability in every later year. The simulation measures the size of that reduction over the six-year horizon.
 
\section{\label{sec:data}Data and Calibration}
 
The simulation draws on three inputs: a rotation-conditioned yield distribution, a price distribution, and operating costs, each estimated from a distinct source. Section~\ref{subsec:panel} describes the Illinois field panel; Section~\ref{subsec:prices_costs} the price and cost series; Section~\ref{subsec:calibration} the credit-cycle calibration.
 
\subsection{\label{subsec:panel}The Illinois field panel}
 
The yield panel covers every corn-planted field in Illinois from 2008 to 2022, a span chosen to balance the availability of high-resolution yield maps (which begin in 2008) and the need for a window long enough to estimate rotation-conditional moments in sparse cells. The panel is constructed by intersecting four geospatial layers at the field-by-year level: the Cropland Data Layer (CDL)\footnote{\url{https://www.nass.usda.gov/Research_and_Science/Cropland/SARS1a.php}} for crop identification and rotation history, the QDANN yield-mapping product \citep{Ma2024} for realized yields, GRIDMET \citep{Abatzoglou2013}\footnote{\url{https://www.climatologylab.org/gridmet.html}} and TerraClimate \citep{Abatzoglou2018} for weather covariates, and gSSURGO\footnote{\url{https://www.nrcs.usda.gov/resources/data-and-reports/gridded-soil-survey-geographic-gssurgo-database}} for soil characteristics. Soybean yields come from a parallel panel of soybean fields. The soybean generators use the same region $\times$ zone cells and the same estimation as the corn generators, with the rotation state defined by the six-year history ending in soybeans.
 
Field boundaries are defined as contiguous CDL pixels of the same crop in a given year, aggregated to a stable spatial unit using the Common Land Unit (CLU) layer \citep{Yan2016}. We construct six-year rotation histories by intersecting each field's geometry with the CDL layer for each of the preceding five years; we drop histories that overlap multiple CDL classes above a small threshold to preserve a clean state definition (Figure~\ref{fig:map_practice}). The resulting panel contains field-year observations with $(s, c, z, Y)$ as the primitive record, where $s$ is the six-year rotation state, $c$ is the climate region, $z$ is the productivity zone, and $Y$ is the QDANN-estimated yield.

\subsection{\label{subsec:prices_costs}Prices and Costs}
 
Harvest-time prices for corn and soybeans are drawn from regional lognormal distributions. Regional mean prices are fixed at \$3.71--\$3.80 per bushel for corn and \$9.57--\$9.70 per bushel for soybeans. The log-volatility is estimated from elevator-level cash prices in Illinois as the standard deviation of elevator-demeaned log harvest-month prices (October for corn, October--November for soybeans) over ten years, which gives 0.204--0.233 for corn and 0.168--0.173 for soybeans. The lognormal choice is conventional in the crop-insurance ratemaking literature and gives a parametric form for the price marginal that the copula can attach to without the cell-by-cell sample-size constraints that bind on the yield side.
 
Operating costs follow the University of Illinois Farmdoc cost-of-production estimates for corn and soybean production on Illinois grain farms. We use 2025 direct costs by region and productivity level: corn after soybeans costs \$452--\$486 per acre and soybeans after corn \$207--\$225 per acre, with the medium-productivity zone set to the average of the high and low zones. Each year's cost is drawn from a lognormal distribution around that level with a log standard deviation of 0.10, independently of prices and yields, and costs are otherwise held constant in real terms over the horizon. The cost premium for corn after corn is not applied in the credit simulation. Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_B} reports robustness to the cost-shock specification, which produces qualitatively identical results.
 
\subsection{\label{subsec:calibration}Credit-cycle calibration}
 
The operating-loan interest rate is set to $i = 9$ percent annualized in the baseline calibration, near the midpoint of the 7--12 percent range reported by the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago's quarterly survey of agricultural lenders over the sample period. The nine-month accrual window corresponds to borrowing in January and repaying in October. The credit ceiling in the rationing analysis (Section~\ref{subsec:peak_demand}) is benchmarked at \$700, \$900, and \$1{,}200 per acre, spanning the range of operating-line limits typical of Farm Credit System and commercial-bank lending to Illinois corn-belt producers during the sample period. The three thresholds form a sensitivity grid, not point estimates; the finding in Section~\ref{subsec:peak_demand} that median peak demand lies between \$488 and \$551 per acre holds across reasonable perturbations of the ceiling values.
 
The delinquency threshold is set at $B_t > 0$ in the baseline (the ``end-balance-positive'' rule): any unpaid balance counts as delinquency, regardless of amount. An alternative rule, ``over-limit'' delinquency ($L_t > $ credit ceiling), is reported in Section~\ref{sec:robustness} as a robustness check and produces broadly similar comparative results across rotation plans at lower absolute levels.
 
\section{\label{sec:simulation}The Simulation Framework}
 
The simulation has three components: a rotation-conditioned yield generator, a price-yield dependence structure, and an operating-credit cash-flow recursion. Each component is standard in the literature; the contribution lies in their combination and in the recursion's preservation of within-year credit flow, which converts a single yield shock into multi-year default risk. Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_A} contains calibration values and computational details.
 
\subsection{\label{subsec:overview}Overview}
 
For each cell $(c,z)$, indexing climate region by productivity zone, rotation plan $r$, and six-year history $h$, the simulation draws $N = 50{,}000$ Monte Carlo paths over a six-year horizon. A path consists of six (price, yield) realizations passed through the credit recursion to produce a sequence of year-end debt balances.

Unless stated otherwise, the initial history is $h = \text{CCCCCC}$: the field has been in continuous corn for the preceding six years. Under this history all three plans plant corn in year 1 from the same agronomic state, so their year-1 delinquency hazards coincide by construction, and the rotation plans carry a continuous-corn penalty into their first years. The measured benefit is therefore the benefit of switching to rotation from a corn-heavy history, not the steady-state benefit of a long-established rotation. The simulation also runs the steady-state history $h = \text{CSCSCS}$, which we do not report here.

The six-year delinquency probability is the share of paths with at least one year-end balance above the delinquency threshold:
\begin{equation}
\Pr[\text{delinquent over 6 yrs} \mid c, z, r, h] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} \mathbb{1}\!\Bigl\{\max_{t \in 1\ldots 6} B^{(n)}_t > 0\Bigr\},
\end{equation}
where $B^{(n)}_t$ is the debt carried out of year $t$ on path $n$, defined recursively in Section~\ref{subsec:recursion}. Per-year hazards, survival functions, and revenue moments are functionals of the same simulated path set.
 
The simulation is forward-looking and policy-conditional: we fix the rotation plan $r$ ex ante (continuous corn, strict alternation, or 2-corn/1-soy) and trace its financial consequences. This inverts the usual dynamic-programming question of \citet{Burt1963} and \citet{Livingston2015}, who solve for the optimal policy. The advantage of the policy-conditional framing is that the resulting probability is directly comparable across plans, which is the quantity a lender or insurer needs.
 
\subsection{\label{subsec:generator}Rotation-conditioned yield generator}
 
Let $Y_t$ denote yield in year $t$ (bu/ac, crop-specific) and let $s_t = (c_{t-5}, c_{t-4}, \ldots, c_{t-1}, c_t)$ denote the six-year rotation state ending in year $t$. The yield generator is the family of conditional distributions:
\begin{equation}
F_{Y \mid s, c, z}, \quad s \in \mathcal{S},\ c \in \mathcal{C},\ z \in \mathcal{Z},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{S}$ is the set of six-year rotation states observed in the panel, $\mathcal{C}$ indexes the three Illinois climate regions, and $\mathcal{Z}$ indexes the three productivity zones.
 
We estimate $F_{Y \mid s, c, z}$ nonparametrically in four steps, separately for each crop and each region $\times$ zone cell. First, we average QDANN yields to the field-year level for field-years whose crop matches. Second, we fit a two-way fixed-effects model with field and year effects. Third, we compute each field-year's residual from the fitted field and year effects. Fourth, for each six-year rotation state $s$ we store the mean fitted value $\mu_s$ and a pool of 20,000 residuals drawn from that state's residuals (without replacement when enough are available, with replacement otherwise). A draw from $F_{Y \mid s, c, z}$ is $\mu_s$ plus a resample from the state's pool, so the rotation effect enters through the state-specific mean and through the pool, whose mean is the part of the state's mean yield that the field and year effects do not absorb. Because the year effects are removed, they are not reintroduced as estimated: the independence runs use the pool as is, and the correlated runs use only its standard deviation, with the systematic share of variance assumed (Section~\ref{subsec:py_corr}). The simulation uses only rotation states observed in the cell's panel.
 
The nonparametric choice is deliberate. Parametric specifications (e.g., Beta or Gamma distributions, the standard in crop-insurance ratemaking) impose tail behavior that the rotation effect plausibly distorts: continuous corn under high-pressure conditions may exhibit fatter lower tails than a Beta or Gamma calibrated on mean and variance would predict. The conditional empirical distribution preserves the data's shape, at the cost of slightly noisier tails in sparse cells. We do not estimate a parametric alternative; Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_B} reports the dispersion of revenue under the nonparametric generator.
 
Given an initial six-year history $h$ and a rotation plan $r$, the state $s_t$ evolves deterministically as a one-period shift: $s_{t} = (c_{t-5}, c_{t-4}, \ldots, c_{t-1}, r_t)$, where $r_t$ is the crop planted in year $t$ under plan $r$ and $(c_{t-5}, \ldots, c_{t-1})$ are the realized crops of the preceding five years. The random component of the simulation enters only through draws from $F_{Y \mid s, c, z}$.
 
\subsection{\label{subsec:py_corr}Price-yield dependence}
 
Let $P_t$ denote the harvest-time market price of the crop planted in year $t$. Prices and yields are negatively correlated: when yields are high across the market, prices tend to fall, and when they are low, prices rise. The methodological question is how much of that hedge applies at the \textit{field level}.
 
\textbf{Source of the correlation inputs.} The dependence inputs are county-level price--yield correlations for corn and for soybeans. We keep the Illinois counties, map each to its climate region, and take the median across counties in the region. This gives corn correlations of $-0.04$ (central), $-0.27$ (northern), and $-0.37$ (southern), and soybean correlations of $-0.12$, $-0.36$, and $-0.29$.
 
Only the systematic part of a field's yield shock can move the market price, so the simulation applies these correlations to a systematic share of the yield variance rather than to the whole shock, as described below.
 
\textbf{Variance decomposition of the yield shock.} The two-way fixed-effects model estimated in Section~\ref{subsec:generator}, $Y_{f,t} = \alpha_f + \gamma_t + \varepsilon_{f,t}$, partitions observed yield into three components, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:decomp}:

The systematic component (the year fixed effect $\hat{\gamma}_t$, capturing common weather, input-price shocks, and aggregate demand conditions) accounts for 62 percent of total yield variance. Only $\hat{\gamma}_t$ can correlate with the market price: when aggregate yields across all Illinois fields are low in year $t$, $\hat{\gamma}_t$ is negative, and the market price tends to be high. The idiosyncratic residual $\varepsilon_{f,t}$ is, by construction, orthogonal to the year effect and therefore uncorrelated with price. The simulation does not feed this panel estimate into the dependence structure; it fixes the systematic share of yield variance at $\kappa = 0.40$, as described next.
 
\textbf{Dependence structure.} In the correlated specification, price and yield are drawn jointly. Let $\mu_s$ and $\sigma_s$ denote the mean yield and the standard deviation of the residual pool for rotation state $s$, and let $\kappa = 0.40$ be the share of yield variance that is systematic. The log price and the systematic yield shock are drawn from a bivariate normal distribution with correlation $\rho_{r,k}$, the regional median county correlation for region $r$ and crop $k$ reported above. The idiosyncratic yield shock is an independent normal draw and is uncorrelated with price:
\begin{equation}
\log P = \log\bar{P} - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_P^2 + \sigma_P\, w, \qquad Y = \max\bigl\{\mu_s + \sqrt{\kappa}\,\sigma_s z_1 + \sqrt{1-\kappa}\,\sigma_s z_2,\; 0\bigr\},
\end{equation}
where $(w, z_1)$ are standard normal with $\text{Corr}(w, z_1) = \rho_{r,k}$ and $z_2$ is an independent standard normal. The correlation between log price and total yield is therefore $\rho_{r,k}\sqrt{\kappa}$, which ranges from $-0.03$ (central corn) to $-0.23$ (southern corn and northern soybeans). Applying $\rho_{r,k}$ to the whole yield shock rather than to its systematic share would overstate the hedge by a factor of $1/\sqrt{\kappa} \approx 1.58$.
 
For comparison, an independence benchmark ($\rho_{r,k} = 0$) isolates the hedge benefit (Section~\ref{subsec:natural_hedge}). In the independence runs the yield is the state mean plus a draw from the empirical residual pool for that state, so the contrast between the two specifications also reflects any departure of the empirical pool from normality.
 
\textbf{What the simulation draws from.} Each simulated path is a single field. The residual pool $\{\hat{\varepsilon}_{f,t}\}$ is what remains of yield after removing both fixed effects. In the independence specification the yield is the state mean plus a draw from this pool. In the correlated specification the yield is the normal draw above, with the same state mean and the standard deviation of the same pool. Prices and costs are drawn independently of the rotation state.
 
For an insurer evaluating the aggregate loss distribution across a portfolio of Illinois fields, the systematic year shock induces cross-field correlation that substantially fattens the portfolio tail. Extending the framework to portfolio insurance pricing would require reintroducing the year effect as a common factor drawn once per simulated year and added to all fields' yields, a straightforward extension left for future work.
 
\subsection{\label{subsec:recursion}Operating-credit cash-flow recursion}
 
The credit module is the paper's central methodological contribution. Standard practice in crop-insurance and farm-finance modeling collapses each year into a single net cash flow $\Pi_t = P_t Y_t - C_t$, where $C_t$ is the year's operating cost. This is correct for present-value calculations, but it loses the within-year credit flow that drives multi-year default. The operating loan that funds spring planting is a contractual obligation that accrues interest from origination to harvest and imposes a discrete repayment event at harvest. A shortfall at that event rolls forward to the next spring's principal at the operating-loan rate, \textit{not} at the risk-free discount rate.
 
We model the credit cycle explicitly. Let $B_t$ denote the debt carried out of year $t$, equivalently the balance entering year $t+1$, with $B_0 = 0$. The recursion in year $t$ is:
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
L_t &= C_t + B_{t-1} && \text{(1) spring origination}\\
H_t &= L_t \cdot (1 + i/12)^{9} && \text{(2) nine-month accrual}\\
R_t &= P_t Y_t && \text{(3) harvest revenue}\\
M_t &= \min(R_t, H_t) && \text{(4) repayment}\\
B_t &= H_t - M_t && \text{(5) carry forward}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
where $L_t$ is the year-$t$ operating loan, $H_t$ is the debt outstanding at harvest, $R_t$ is realized revenue, $M_t$ is the payment, and $i$ is the nominal annual operating-loan rate (9\% in the baseline), compounded monthly. We define delinquency in year $t$ as $B_t > 0$. We report robustness to the interest rate (6 and 12 percent) and to a six- or twelve-month accrual window in Appendix~\ref{subsec:rate_sens}.
 
Step~(5) of the recursion is where a bad year becomes a bad run. A revenue shortfall of $S$ in year $t$ enters year $t+1$'s principal as $S \cdot (1 + i/12)^{9} \approx 1.07 S$; a modest year-1 deficit becomes a non-trivial year-2 principal even if year-2's yield is average. The compounding is mechanical; it requires no risk aversion or behavioral structure.
 
The recursion has no consumption smoothing, no off-farm income, and no working-capital buffer beyond what is implicit in step~(4)'s $\min(R_t, H_t)$. A buffer would attenuate the delinquency response to any given shock but would not change the relative ranking of rotation plans. The simulation therefore provides an upper bound on the effect of rotation on delinquency rather than a calibrated point estimate; the appendix's robustness checks incorporate a proportional working-capital buffer and show the rotation effect is robust to its inclusion.
 
\subsection{\label{subsec:decompositions}Outcomes and decompositions}
 
The simulation produces three classes of output objects. The first is the delinquency probability itself, in both six-year cumulative form (Section~\ref{subsec:delinq_prob}) and per-year hazard form (Section~\ref{subsec:sawtooth}). The hazard shows that intermittent debt resets drive the cumulative effect.
 
The second is a \textbf{variance decomposition} of gross revenue. Holding the joint distribution of $(P, Y)$ fixed, we apply the standard first-order (delta-method) decomposition of $\text{Var}(P Y)$ into the share attributable to price-only variation, to yield-only variation, and to the covariance term; the second-order term $\text{Var}(P)\text{Var}(Y)$ is omitted. Because the copula imposes negative dependence, the covariance term is negative; we report shares of gross variance ($\text{Var}(P) E[Y]^2$ and $\text{Var}(Y) E[P]^2$) so the shares sum to 100 percent.
 
The third is a \textbf{Shapley decomposition} of the probability of delinquency into contributions from price and yield uncertainty. Variance shares describe how revenue \textit{varies} but not how often the variation crosses the default threshold. Following \citet{Shorrocks2013}, the Shapley value for source $s \in \{P, Y\}$ is:
\begin{equation}
\phi_s = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl[V(\{s\}) - V(\emptyset)\bigr] + \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl[V(\{P,Y\}) - V(\{P,Y\} \setminus \{s\})\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $V(\mathcal{A})$ is the delinquency probability when sources in $\mathcal{A}$ are stochastic and sources outside $\mathcal{A}$ are fixed at their unconditional means. The Shapley values sum to $V(\{P,Y\}) - V(\emptyset)$, the total delinquency-causing variation, with no interaction-term residual. In the baseline calibration $V(\emptyset) = 0$: with both price and yield fixed at their means, revenue covers the accrued obligation in every year, so the two Shapley values sum to the delinquency probability itself.
 
\section{\label{sec:results}Results}
 
Section~\ref{sec:results} is organized around four questions. Why should rotation affect farm finances at all: the agronomic mechanisms (Section~\ref{subsec:agronomic_benefits})? How do those mechanisms transmit through the operating-credit cash-flow process into measurable financial outcomes (Section~\ref{subsec:transmission_channel})? How large are the resulting differences in delinquency risk, in cumulative, per-year, and loan-demand terms (Sections~\ref{subsec:delinq_prob}-\ref{subsec:peak_demand})? And how much do the natural hedge and price-yield correlation moderate or substitute for those effects (Sections~\ref{subsec:natural_hedge}-\ref{subsec:stress_test})?
 
\subsection{\label{subsec:agronomic_benefits}Agronomic benefits of crop rotation}
 
The case for crop rotation rests on two distinct agronomic channels: soil health benefits that improve expected yields and reduce yield-tail risk, and temporal diversification that reduces the farm's biological and agrochemical exposure to any single commodity.
 
\textbf{Soil health and yield effects.} The yield benefit of corn following soybean rather than corn following corn is one of the best-replicated findings in agronomy. \citet{Bullock1992} documents a consistent yield advantage of 5--20 percent for corn in rotation relative to monoculture; \citet{Seifert2017} estimate a 4.3 percent penalty for continuous corn using satellite data across hundreds of thousands of Midwestern fields. The factor-of-four gap between the two reflects differences in design and era: Bullock reviews rotation studies, while Seifert et al.\ estimate the penalty from field-level observations of farmer-managed corn, so the smaller figure plausibly reflects modern hybrids and management. We treat 5--20 percent as the range for the mean-yield channel; the debt-reset mechanism below does not depend on it. The mechanism is multifactorial: soybeans fix atmospheric nitrogen through their root-nodule symbiosis with \textit{Bradyrhizobium}, supplying 50--150 lb/ac of plant-available nitrogen to the subsequent corn crop and reducing synthetic fertilizer requirements. Rotation also interrupts the pest and pathogen cycles that accumulate under monoculture: corn rootworm (\textit{Diabrotica} spp.), northern corn leaf blight, and gray leaf spot all depend on corn residue and are biologically suppressed when corn is absent for a year. \citet{Bowles2020} synthesize 347 site-years of rotation experiments and show that diversified rotations reduce yield losses in drought years by 14--89 percent and lower the probability of yields falling below the 10th percentile, exactly the region that triggers operating-loan default.
 
Beyond mean yields, diverse rotations improve soil organic matter accumulation, water infiltration, and aggregate stability over time, reducing yield sensitivity to precipitation extremes. Any compression of the left tail of yields translates directly into reduced delinquency risk through the cash-flow channel in Section~\ref{subsec:transmission_channel}.
 
\textbf{Temporal diversification.} A farm alternating between corn and soybeans faces two distinct input cost profiles, two price series imperfectly correlated at the farm-gate level, and two distinct growing-season weather sensitivities. Corn is most vulnerable to moisture stress during pollination in late July, soybeans during pod fill in August; a weather event that devastates corn may be less damaging to soybeans and vice versa. This temporal diversification reduces the probability of consecutive catastrophic revenue shortfalls, which is the sequence that drives the multi-year delinquency cascade. A single bad corn year is survivable; two or three consecutive bad corn years, each rolling forward an unpaid balance that enlarges the next spring loan, are not.
 
Temporal diversification also operates through input costs. Soybean production costs are less than half of corn production costs in the Farmdoc data (44--49 percent across regions and zones; Section~\ref{subsec:prices_costs}), so a soybean year is planted on a smaller spring loan regardless of how the prior corn year resolved. This cost differential directly drives the debt-reset mechanism in Section~\ref{subsec:delinq_prob}: the smaller originating loan means a given revenue draw is more likely to retire the full obligation, preventing rollover.
 
\subsection{\label{subsec:transmission_channel}From agronomy to farm finances: the transmission channel}
 
The agronomic benefits in Section~\ref{subsec:agronomic_benefits} must pass through the operating-credit cash-flow process to become financial outcomes. Three properties of the credit recursion (Section~\ref{subsec:recursion}) govern how agronomy enters the financial outcome.
 
\textbf{Mean yield matters through $R_t$.} A higher expected yield raises expected revenue, increasing the probability that $R_t$ exceeds the full obligation $(C_t + B_{t-1})(1 + i/12)^{9}$. The corn-after-corn yield penalty directly reduces $E[R_t]$ for continuous-corn producers, making revenue shortfalls more probable year after year. The 5--20 percent yield difference documented in the agronomic literature (roughly 10--40 bu/ac at typical Illinois yields) corresponds to a revenue difference of \$40-\$160 per acre at average corn prices. A persistent shortfall of this magnitude ensures that $B_{t-1}$ entering the next year is systematically positive, enlarging the obligation the next year's revenue must cover.
 
\textbf{The left tail matters more than the mean.} The delinquency indicator is binary: either revenue covers the full obligation, or it does not. For multi-year delinquency, what matters is not expected shortfall but the probability that the shortfall is large enough to leave a positive year-end balance. Left-tail compression, documented by \citet{Bowles2020}, slows the rollover chain before it starts. This is why the rotation effect in the simulation is concentrated in the tails of the delinquency distribution rather than at the median.
 
\textbf{Cost structure interacts with the yield distribution through the debt reset.} The soybean year is planted on a lower spring loan $C_t$, so the obligation $(C_t + B_{t-1})(1 + i/12)^{9}$ is smaller by design, regardless of how much $B_{t-1}$ accumulated in prior corn years. In sufficiently bad corn years, no plausible yield draw will cover a large accumulated $B_{t-1}$; but the soybean year creates a year in which the obligation is small enough to be covered at moderate revenue realizations. This is the debt reset: not a guaranteed zero balance, but a structural reduction in the obligation that makes retirement of the carried debt likely even without exceptional yields.
 
The simulation does not separate the cost channel from the yield channel by construction: rotation plans differ in both the cost path and the rotation-conditional yield distributions. The mechanism above is the interpretation the hazard patterns support, namely near-zero delinquency in every soybean year, rather than a decomposition of the effect. Sections~\ref{subsec:delinq_prob}-\ref{subsec:sawtooth} present the quantitative results.

Strict alternation cuts those probabilities by 12.8 to 26.6 percentage points, or by 36 to 46 percent in relative terms: 17.5 percent versus 32.1 percent in central-high, 16.0 percent versus 29.1 percent in northern-high, and 47.6 percent versus 74.2 percent in southern-low. In 7 of the 9 cells the relative reduction is 41 percent or more, close to halving the multi-year default risk of a continuous-corn operation.
 
The 2-corn/1-soy plan falls between continuous corn and strict alternation in every cell, at 20--59 percent. More soybean years mean more debt-reset opportunities, but the relationship is not strictly linear in soybean frequency, as Section~\ref{subsec:sawtooth} shows.
 
Productivity zone moves the bars a great deal. Under continuous corn in the northern region, the six-year probability rises from 29.1 percent in the high zone to 42.4 percent in the medium zone and 68.4 percent in the low zone; the corresponding spreads from the high to the low zone are 22.7 points in the central region and 46.3 points in the south. The rotation effect also grows as productivity falls: the gap between continuous corn and strict alternation rises from 13.1 to 25.1 points in the north, from 14.6 to 22.6 points in the center, and from 12.8 to 26.6 points in the south. Rotation lowers delinquency in every cell, and the reduction is largest in the low-productivity cells, where expected revenue sits closest to the obligation.

The simulated yield generators contain almost no mean-yield advantage for rotation. After the common year effect is removed from each rotation state's mean, the simulated mean corn yield after soybeans in strict alternation differs from continuous corn by between $-0.9$ and $+0.9$ bu/ac in every cell, and the within-field component of that gap (carried by the residual pools) is 0 to 1.5 bu/ac. The reduction in delinquency reported here is therefore not driven by a difference in mean corn yields. It operates through the cost path (the soybean year's smaller loan), the debt-reset mechanism, and any differences in the shape of the conditional yield distributions; the simulation does not separate these channels, and the results should not be read as adding to the corn-after-corn yield penalty documented in the agronomic literature.
 
\subsection{\label{subsec:sawtooth}Per-year hazard: the sawtooth}
 
Figure~\ref{fig:fig_p_year_all} plots the year-by-year default probability for each plan across all nine region $\times$ zone cells.
 
\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=16cm]{figs/fig_p_year_all.png}
\caption{Soy years reset the debt cycle: the sawtooth}
\label{fig:fig_p_year_all}
\end{figure}
 
Continuous corn produces a roughly flat hazard with a mild upward drift as debt accumulates: in every cell the year-6 hazard exceeds the year-1 hazard (for example 5.6 to 6.1 percent in northern-high, 6.2 to 6.9 percent in central-high, and 17.4 to 21.7 percent in northern-low). The hazard stays elevated in every year, with no periodic relief. The rotation plans differ in kind, not just in level. They produce a \textbf{sawtooth}: the hazard collapses to near zero in soybean-harvest years (at most 1.2 percent under strict alternation and 2.0 percent under 2-corn/1-soy, across all cells) and rebuilds during corn years. Under strict alternation, the flat segments fall on years 2, 4, and 6. Under 2-corn/1-soy, they fall on years 3 and 6.
 
The mechanism is the debt reset. Soybean production costs are less than half of corn costs in the Farmdoc data (44--49 percent), so a soybean year originates on a smaller spring loan, making the harvest-time obligation easier to clear even in a mediocre revenue year. When the soybean harvest retires the accumulated rollover balance from prior corn years, the farm enters the next corn run with near-zero carried debt and starts the cycle over. The six-year cumulative probability is not a smoothed average of annual risks. It results from intermittent arrests in a compounding process.
 
The financial benefit identified here is distinct from the yield benefit documented by \citet{Bullock1992} and \citet{Seifert2017} in where it shows up: the hazard falls to near zero in the soybean year itself, which a mean-yield effect on later corn crops cannot produce. The simulation does not run a counterfactual that holds corn yield distributions identical across plans, so we do not report how much of the six-year reduction comes from the debt reset and how much from the yield benefit.
 
 
The survival curves implied by these hazards are discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:survival}.
 
\subsection{\label{subsec:peak_demand}Peak operating-loan demand}
 
\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=16cm]{figs/fig_peak_loan_all.png}
\caption{Peak loan demand stays under common credit limits}
\label{fig:fig_peak_loan_all}
\end{figure}
 
A related concern for lenders is not just whether a borrower becomes delinquent, but whether the demanded operating loan exceeds a credit ceiling the lender is willing to extend. Figure~\ref{fig:fig_peak_loan_all} reports the distribution of peak gross spring loan demand ($L_t = C_t + B_{t-1}$) over the six-year horizon, with representative credit ceilings of \$700, \$900, and \$1{,}200 per acre overlaid.
 
In every cell, the median peak loan lies between \$488 and \$550 per acre, well below all three ceiling values. Rationing is therefore rare under all three plans at any calibrated limit: under continuous corn the probability that peak demand exceeds \$700 is 0.1--3.5 percent across cells, exceeds \$900 is at most 0.12 percent, and exceeds \$1{,}200 is zero. The rotation effect on the \textit{distribution} of peak demand is nonetheless economically meaningful. Continuous corn produces a right tail with a 99th percentile of \$630--\$777, while strict alternation lowers the median peak by \$22--\$37 per acre and the 99th percentile by \$40--\$165, and the probability of exceeding \$700 is at most 0.03 percent. Rotation lowers both the \textit{probability} of delinquency and the \textit{exposure} a lender faces if a delinquency does occur. A lender incorporating rotation history would price risk lower for rotation borrowers on both dimensions simultaneously.
 
\subsection{\label{subsec:natural_hedge}The natural hedge: rotation and correlation are partial substitutes}
 
Figure~\ref{fig:fig_p_debt_all} isolates the natural-hedge effect by reporting mean year-end carried debt under two specifications: the baseline correlated simulation (``hedge active'') and a counterfactual independent simulation (``no hedge''). The vertical gap is the hedge benefit.
 
\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=16cm]{figs/fig_p_debt_all.png}
\caption{The natural hedge mostly helps continuous corn}
\label{fig:fig_p_debt_all}
\end{figure}
 
The hedge gap is widest for continuous corn, averaging \$1.7 per acre of year-end debt across cells and years (\$0.5--\$3.9 depending on the cell); it is smaller for 2-corn/1-soy (\$0.1--\$2.3, mean \$0.8) and smallest for strict alternation (\$0.00--\$1.23, mean \$0.4), where it is essentially zero in the central region. Rotation and the natural hedge are \textbf{partial substitutes} in the cash-flow space, not complements. Continuous corn benefits from the hedge because nothing else arrests the debt-rollover process. Rotation plans benefit less because they have already accomplished what the hedge would otherwise do, namely periodically driving carried debt to zero in soybean years, so the hedge has little remaining buffering work to perform.
 
In a one-year revenue-protection setting, the natural hedge is unambiguously beneficial and additive to whatever other risk management the farmer employs. In the multi-year credit-risk setting, the hedge is most useful to the borrower with no other instrument (the continuous-corn borrower) and less useful to the borrower who rotates. Field-level hedge magnitudes estimated on single-year revenue data may therefore \textbf{overstate} the hedge's marginal contribution to multi-year financial resilience, because they do not net out the rotation channel operating in parallel \citep{Finger2012, Ramsey2019}.
 
\subsection{\label{subsec:hedge_benefit}The hedge benefit in delinquency probability space}
 
Figure~\ref{fig:fig_p_bar_corr} translates the same comparison into the delinquency probability space, the paper's headline outcome. For each of the nine region $\times$ zone cells, it plots the six-year delinquency probability under the independent specification (faded bar, no hedge) and the correlated specification (solid bar, hedge active), side by side for each rotation plan. The gap between the two bars within a plan is the hedge's marginal contribution to delinquency reduction.
 
\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=16cm]{figs/fig_p_bar_corr.png}
\caption{Price-yield correlation dampens the effects of rotations}
\label{fig:fig_p_bar_corr}
\end{figure}
 
\textbf{The hedge benefit is small.} In 24 of the 27 cell-plan combinations the solid bar is shorter than the faded bar; the exceptions are the three central cells under strict alternation, where the difference is $-0.4$ to $-0.1$ percentage points, within Monte Carlo noise. But the gap is narrow. In the northern and central rows, the hedge reduces continuous-corn delinquency probability by 1.7--4.4 percentage points (1.7--2.8 in the central region and 2.8--4.4 in the northern region); for 2-corn/1-soy the reduction is 0.6--1.2 points in the central region and 1.6--2.9 in the northern region. Outside the southern region (the exception discussed below), the hedge never moves the needle by more than 4.4 percentage points. Against baseline continuous-corn probabilities of 29--68 percent in the northern and central cells, a reduction of this size is modest.
 
\textbf{The hedge benefit is largest for continuous corn and smallest for strict alternation.} Within each region the average gap narrows progressively across CCCCCC, CCSCCS, and CSCSCS (northern 3.7, 2.2, and 1.3 points; central 2.1, 0.8, and $-0.3$; southern 5.3, 4.2, and 3.1). It is near zero for CSCSCS in the central region and between 0.8 and 1.7 points in the northern region. A rotation plan that resets carried debt in soybean years has already eliminated much of the rollover balance that the hedge would otherwise partially protect against, so the price-yield correlation has less to offset.
 
\textbf{The southern region is the partial exception.} The hedge gap in the south row is visibly wider (3.3--6.4 percentage points for continuous corn, 4.0--4.4 for 2-corn/1-soy, and still 2.9--3.4 for strict alternation) than in the north and central rows. The correlation inputs differ by region: the southern corn correlation ($-0.37$) is the strongest of the three, against $-0.27$ in the north and $-0.04$ in the central region (Section~\ref{subsec:py_corr}), which directly enlarges the hedge in the south and shrinks it in the central region. The regional ordering of the hedge (central smallest, southern largest) therefore follows the county-level correlation inputs rather than any feature of the credit recursion.
 
Figures~\ref{fig:fig_p_debt_all} and~\ref{fig:fig_p_bar_corr} make the same argument from two angles: the hedge reduces year-end carried debt most for continuous corn and least for rotation plans, and this debt reduction translates into a modest and unevenly distributed reduction in delinquency probability. Both effects are modest because price risk dominates revenue variance (Section~\ref{subsec:var_decomp}) and the hedge can only offset the covariance term; both effects shrink with rotation because rotation preempts part of the rollover channel through which the hedge would otherwise operate.
 
\subsection{\label{subsec:stress_test}Yield-shock stress test: the hedge under a localized corn shortfall}
 
Figure~\ref{fig:fig_yield_compare_corr_vs_indep} isolates the hedge's role under a specific shock: a $-12$ percent corn yield shortfall applied only in year 3, with no shock applied in the other five years. The experiment runs under both the correlated and independent price specifications; the gap between the two rows within each panel is the natural-hedge benefit conditional on the shock, rather than averaged across the full simulation. Because year 3 is a soybean year under the CCSCCS plan, CCSCCS is a null control: a corn yield shock in a soybean year mechanically produces no delinquency response, a clean within-figure placebo.
 
\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=16cm]{figs/fig_yield_compare_corr_vs_indep.png}
\caption{Yield-shock stress test}
\label{fig:fig_yield_compare_corr_vs_indep}
\end{figure}
 
The top panel reports the change in six-year delinquency probability relative to the no-shock baseline (positive values indicate worsening). The bottom panel reports the change in present value of net cash flow over the six-year horizon (\$/acre; negative values indicate a loss).
 
\textbf{The delinquency response is large for CCCCCC and CSCSCS, and zero for CCSCCS.} Under continuous corn, a single $-12$ percent yield shock in year 3 raises six-year delinquency probability by 6.3--8.8 percentage points across all cells under the correlated specification, and 4.9--7.2 under the independent specification. The correlated-independent gap runs from $-0.3$ to $+2.1$ percentage points, and the correlated response is the larger of the two in seven of the nine cells. The hedge therefore does not dampen the response to this shock; the correlated specification starts from a lower baseline delinquency probability and so leaves more paths free to flip, the same base-rate mechanism discussed below. Under strict alternation, year 3 is a corn year, so the shock hits; the delinquency response is even larger, running 7.4--11.8 percentage points across cells under the correlated specification and 7.3--10.9 under the independent one. Year 3 is the first corn year after the initial year-2 soybean reset, so the field enters it with near-zero carried debt, but a severe enough yield shortfall still generates a non-trivial probability of a first delinquency event, which the year-4 soybean year will then be in a position to arrest. The CCSCCS cells are uniformly near zero ($-0.2$ to $+0.7$ percentage points), as expected for a placebo applied in a soybean year.
 
\textbf{The PV cash response is symmetric across plans and specifications.} Both plans planting corn in year 3 show a cash loss of \$60--\$89 per acre, similar across the correlated and independent rows. The hedge does not materially protect cash flow: the shock is a single-year idiosyncratic yield draw that does not move national supply enough to produce a meaningful price offset at the field level. The CCSCCS placebo shows near-zero cash changes of $\pm\$5$ per acre.
 
The asymmetry between the two panels is the key finding. The PV cash response is roughly equal across plans and price models: a $-12$ percent yield shock costs approximately the same dollar amount regardless of rotation or hedge status. But the delinquency response differs substantially across plans, and the reason lies in the measure rather than in the cash loss. The six-year delinquency probability is the share of paths that record at least one delinquency, so a shock can change the outcome only for paths that have not already been delinquent. Under CCCCCC a substantial share of paths have recorded a first delinquency before year 3, leaving the shock fewer fresh paths to flip; under CSCSCS the year-2 soybean reset leaves most paths still free of delinquency when the shock arrives, so a shock of the same cash size flips more of them. The measure is therefore sensitive to when in the path a shock lands. Delinquency probability and cash-flow loss are not the same object: the \textit{path} the debt takes through the credit recursion matters, not just the terminal net present value. Rotation produces fields that are simultaneously more financially resilient on average (lower six-year delinquency, as documented in Section~\ref{subsec:delinq_prob}) and more sensitive to a point shock in a corn year, because more of their paths are still delinquency-free when the shock arrives. A lender underwriting a rotation borrower therefore faces a different \textit{shape} of credit risk: lower persistent default probability but a sharper single-year spike, compared to the continuous-corn borrower's flatter, more slowly compounding hazard.
 
\section{\label{sec:robustness}Decompositions and robustness}
 
Rotation cuts delinquency; the natural hedge is a modest substitute. What source of uncertainty drives the residual risk that rotation does not eliminate? The variance decomposition (Section~\ref{subsec:var_decomp}) attributes gross revenue variance to price and yield. The Shapley decomposition (Section~\ref{subsec:shapley}) attributes delinquency probability to the same two sources. Together they pin down why the hedge is modest: price uncertainty dominates both variance and delinquency causation, so the hedge has little room to operate.
 
\subsection{\label{subsec:var_decomp}Variance decomposition}
 
For each plan-region-zone cell, we decompose the variance of annual gross revenue $R = P Y$ into the share attributable to price-only variation ($\text{Var}(P) \cdot E[Y]^2$ as a share of total gross variance) and the share attributable to yield-only variation ($\text{Var}(Y) \cdot E[P]^2$ as a share of total gross variance). The covariance term $2 E[P] E[Y] \text{Cov}(P, Y)$ is negative under the calibrated dependence structure and is reported separately as the hedge magnitude.
 
\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=16cm]{figs/fig_revenue_variance_decomp.png}
\caption{Price dominates revenue variance}
\label{fig:fig_revenue_variance_decomp}
\end{figure}
 
Figure~\ref{fig:fig_revenue_variance_decomp} reports the result. The price share is 82--96 percent of gross variance across plans, regions, and zones; the yield share is 4--18 percent. The split varies with productivity zone more than with plan: the price share is highest in the northern-high cells (96 percent), 89--94 percent in the other high-productivity cells, 85--93 percent in the medium cells, and 82--86 percent in the low-productivity cells of every region. Within a cell the three plans differ by at most 4 percentage points, so rotation plans change the level of revenue risk far more than its \textit{composition}. The price variance implied by the calibrated log-volatilities dominates the empirical yield variance for Illinois corn and soybeans by approximately the same ratio; the simulation inherits an asymmetry already present in its inputs.
 
Even if price and yield were perfectly negatively correlated, the hedge would offset only the price-yield covariance, at most a few percentage points of total variance, leaving the dominant price-driven variance intact. Rotation, which operates through the within-year debt timing rather than through revenue smoothing, sidesteps this limitation entirely.
 
\subsection{\label{subsec:shapley}Shapley decomposition of delinquency probability}
 
A 50--50 variance split between price and yield is fully consistent with a 99--1 split in default causation if price shocks live in the tail that triggers rollover while yield shocks do not. We decompose delinquency probability directly using the Shapley value $\phi_s$ defined in Section~\ref{subsec:decompositions}, following \citet{Shapley1953} as applied to risk-decomposition problems by \citet{Shorrocks2013} and \citet{Israeli2007}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=16cm]{figs/fig_shapley_price_share.png}
\caption{Shapley values: price drives nearly all delinquency}
\label{fig:fig_shapley_price_share}
\end{figure}
 
The price share of delinquency causation ranges from 83.7 percent in the southern-low cell under continuous corn to 101.8 percent in the northern-high cell under 2-corn/1-soy. Shares above 100 percent occur when the Shapley value of yield is slightly negative, that is, when yield variation alone does not add delinquency risk beyond what price variation creates. The share rises with productivity: it is 84--91 percent in the low zones, 94--100 percent in the medium zones, and 97--102 percent in the high zones. Yield uncertainty contributes most in the low-productivity cells, where its Shapley share reaches up to 16 percent; in absolute terms it accounts for 1.0 of the 32.1 percentage points of delinquency probability in central-high under continuous corn.
 
The Shapley share is \textbf{more lopsided} than the variance share in most cells (for example 96.8 versus 94.1 percent in central-high under continuous corn, and 88.5 versus 84.4 percent in central-low; the difference reaches 11 points in southern-high), and the ranking across zones is the same. Yield shocks, while accounting for 4--18 percent of revenue variance, tend not to generate the tail revenue draws that trigger rollover. Yield distributions are bounded below by physical floors (a zero-yield outcome is exceptional in the Illinois corn belt), while price distributions have longer left tails. Threshold-crossing events disproportionately arise from price shocks. The decomposition explains why the natural hedge is modest: it can offset the small yield-driven component of delinquency, but the dominant price-driven component is unhedged by construction.
 
\subsection{\label{subsec:survival}Survival curves: when does the rotation effect materialize?}
 
Figure~\ref{fig:fig_p_surv_all} plots $\Pr[\text{no delinquency by year } t]$ for each plan across all nine region $\times$ zone cells. The step structure mirrors the sawtooth: survival drops in corn years and holds flat in soybean years. By year 2 CSCSCS lies 5.0--16.1 percentage points above CCCCCC (mean 9.1), and by year 3 (the end of the first full corn-soy cycle) 4.9--13.9 points (mean 8.3); the gap is somewhat smaller in year 3 than in year 2 because year 3 is a corn year under strict alternation. It widens to 9.5--23.9 points by year 4 and 12.8--26.6 points by year 6 (mean 19.1). The earliest delinquency events, occurring in the first corn run before any soybean reset, matter most for the cumulative probability. The separation is durable: continuous corn provides no debt-reset mechanism to recover from early delinquency. By year 6, strict alternation preserves 52--85 percent of fields in solvency across regions and zones, while continuous corn retains only 26--72 percent.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=16cm]{figs/fig_p_surv_all.png}
\caption{Survival curves show where rotation pulls farms apart}
\label{fig:fig_p_surv_all}
\end{figure}
 
Once a field enters the delinquency state, the carried balance enlarges the following year's spring principal, raising the revenue threshold required to return $B_{t+1}$ to zero. Without a soybean year to break the chain, this threshold rises faster than the unconditional mean of revenue, so the conditional probability of a second consecutive delinquency exceeds the unconditional annual hazard. Survival under continuous corn decays faster than a flat-hazard Kaplan-Meier curve would predict; survival under rotation is approximately piecewise flat between resets.
 
A survival gap of 5--16 percentage points is already visible within the first loan-renewal cycle (years 2 and 3). A lender observing a borrower's first two or three years of operating-loan history is already observing the consequence of the rotation choice: the rotation borrower arrives at year-3 renewal with a near-zero carried balance, while the continuous-corn borrower may be carrying a compounded deficit from one or more prior shortfalls. Rotation history observable in CDL imagery predicts not only the eventual six-year default probability but also the early trajectory of the credit relationship.
 
Under the alternative over-limit delinquency rule, absolute delinquency levels fall but the plan ranking and sawtooth pattern both survive. Figure~\ref{fig:fig_shapley_abs} in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_B} reports the absolute Shapley values underlying the normalized shares in Figure~\ref{fig:fig_shapley_price_share}, confirming that the price-driven component dominates not just relatively but in absolute terms.
 
\section{\label{sec:implications}Implications}
 
For \textbf{lenders}, rotation history is an observable, verifiable risk factor that current underwriting does not price. Two farms with identical balance sheets but different six-year rotation states face six-year delinquency probabilities that differ by a factor of 1.6 to 1.8: 32.1 percent versus 17.5 percent in the central-high Illinois area, with similar ratios in the other cells; CDL-derived rotation histories are publicly available at the field level, requiring no additional borrower disclosure, and the conditioning effect on delinquency is robust across the simulation grid. A credit-scoring model incorporating rotation alongside conventional balance-sheet measures would assign strictly different scores to the two otherwise-identical farms, in a direction the simulation argues is correct.
 
For \textbf{insurers}, the multi-year delinquency object identified here is not what Revenue Protection (RP) products price. RP premiums are calibrated on the single-year loss-cost ratio, approximating $E[\max(0, \text{guarantee} - \text{revenue})]$ from a one-year distribution. The single-year objective does not see the rollover channel that converts a year-1 shock into a multi-year default cascade. A corn-heavy producer with a high one-year revenue distribution and a poor multi-year survival distribution may therefore appear cheap to insure on the single-year metric and expensive on the multi-year metric. RP premiums calibrated on single-year loss ratios systematically understate the \textit{insurance value} of rotation, since rotation's largest financial benefit accrues specifically to the multi-year delinquency dimension that RP does not see. To the extent that the federal crop insurance program induces greater debt use \citep{IfftKuethe2013}, it does so under a rate structure that does not reward the rotation choice that would reduce multi-year ruin.
 
For \textbf{extension and policy}, the financial case for rotation now parallels the agronomic and soil-health cases. Rotation's documented benefits for soil organic carbon, nitrogen-use efficiency, and pest pressure \citep{Bullock1992} are matched here by a balance-sheet benefit that is independent of the agronomic channel and additive. A reduction of 13 to 27 percentage points in six-year delinquency probability is an immediately legible benefit to the producer, regardless of whether the producer values the soil-health benefits. Conservation programs that promote rotation, cover crops, and reduced tillage now have a balance-sheet argument to add to the soil-health argument. Rotation diversity across the Midwest has been declining for a century, fastest on the most productive ground, driven partly by the infrastructure of corn demand \citep{socolar2021}. The agronomic argument has not been sufficient to halt this trend. A financial argument visible to lenders and insurers (the parties who actually price farm credit risk) is a candidate countervailing lever, operating not through producer education but through the price of credit itself.
 
The case for incorporating rotation into underwriting is one of risk-pricing accuracy, not behavioral mandate. The simulation does not model the reasons a farmer might rationally choose continuous corn (off-farm income, livestock integration, non-standard cost structures). A lender or insurer that prices a continuous-corn borrower at the same premium as an otherwise identical rotation borrower misprices risk, regardless of whether continuous corn is the right choice for that farmer.
 
\section{\label{sec:conclusion}Conclusion}
 
This paper develops a simulation framework that combines rotation-conditioned yield distributions with an explicit operating-credit cash-flow recursion to produce multi-year delinquency probabilities for Illinois corn-belt farms. The rotation-yield literature stops at yield; the dynamic-crop-choice literature stops at the optimal policy; the farm-credit-risk literature treats yield as a single aggregate input. The combination delivers an empirical object, the rotation-conditional six-year delinquency probability, that current credit and insurance models do not produce.
 
Three findings emerge. Rotation reduces six-year delinquency probability by 13 to 27 percentage points relative to continuous corn, largest in the low-productivity cells and concentrated in soybean-harvest years through a debt-reset mechanism; the per-year hazard exhibits a sawtooth pattern that distinguishes this mechanism from any uniform risk-smoothing explanation. Price uncertainty dominates both revenue variance (82--96 percent) and delinquency causation (roughly 84--100 percent), so the natural price-yield hedge does little on its own (at most 6.4 percentage points in any cell); the hedge is most useful for the continuous-corn borrower who has no other risk-management instrument and matters least for the rotation borrower. The rotation effect arrives early: survival curves show a 5--14 percentage-point separation between continuous corn and strict alternation by year 3, within the first corn-soy cycle, widening to 13--27 points by year 6, and that gap does not close.
 
Rotation history is therefore a missing variable in the credit-risk and crop-insurance models used by lenders, insurers, and regulators. The variable is observable and verifiable in publicly available CDL imagery, and incorporating it would materially improve risk-pricing accuracy. The case is strongest for multi-year insurance products that price ruin rather than single-year shortfall; the simulation suggests these products would price rotation as a strictly favorable feature of the covered farm.
 
Three extensions are worth pursuing. Endogenizing the rotation choice under a credit-risk-sensitive farmer would speak to the equilibrium response of adoption to a hypothetical credit-pricing reform. A loss-given-default extension would convert the probability into a dollar-denominated expected loss that maps directly into pricing. Extending the framework to a region with more heterogeneous crop options (the southeastern U.S.\ cotton-peanut-soybean rotation, for example) would test whether the debt-reset mechanism generalizes beyond the corn-soybean case.
 
Financial outcomes for farms are path-dependent in ways current models do not capture, and that path dependence is rotation-conditional in ways current credit-risk models do not see. The resulting mispricing has direct implications for an industry (agricultural lending and insurance) that intermediates approximately \$220 billion of non-real-estate farm debt in the United States \citep{usda2026}. The framework produces a number that has not existed before: a rotation-conditional, multi-year delinquency probability that lenders and insurers can observe, price, and act on.

\subsection{\label{sec:comp_details}Computational details}

Monte Carlo paths are drawn at $N = 50{,}000$ per (region $\times$ zone $\times$ plan $\times$ initial history) cell. With this many paths the Monte Carlo standard error of a six-year delinquency probability is at most 0.22 percentage points (at a probability of 0.5), and plans within a cell share a random seed, so plan-to-plan differences use common random numbers. The simulation uses only rotation states observed in each cell's panel; no cells are pooled.

\section{\label{sec:appendix_B}Robustness exercises}

Section \ref{sec:results} establishes the baseline results, and Sections \ref{subsec:var_decomp}--\ref{subsec:shapley} report the primary decomposition and robustness exercises. This appendix collects the secondary robustness exercises that are forward-referenced in the main text. Each exercise perturbs one calibration choice while holding the rest of the simulation fixed and reports whether the qualitative ranking of rotation plans and the central mechanism (the sawtooth) survive.

\subsection{\label{subsec:end_debt}Year-end debt distribution}
Figure \ref{fig:fig_p_debt_all} in the main text reports mean year-end carried debt under two price-yield dependence specifications (correlated and independent) to isolate the natural-hedge effect. We additionally examine the 90th and 99th percentiles of year-end carried debt under the baseline (correlated) specification, to check whether rotation bounds the upper tail of carried debt, not just its mean.

This distributional check surfaces two features that an aggregate-only view would miss. First, the \textit{mean} debt path under continuous corn rises over the horizon in every cell, though not strictly monotonically in the four cells with the smallest balances (year-to-year dips of at most \$0.19 per acre in northern-high, northern-medium, central-high, and southern-high). It reaches \$2.9--\$4.3 per acre by year 6 in the high-productivity zones and \$11.0--\$21.3 in the low-productivity zones. The two rotation plans trace sawtooth paths that return close to zero in soybean years (the lowest mean debt under strict alternation is \$0--\$0.5 per acre, depending on the cell) and rebuild during corn years, with maxima below the continuous-corn level in every cell. Second, the upper tail separates the plans more cleanly than the mean. At year 6 the 99th percentile of carried debt under continuous corn is \$89--\$234 per acre across cells, against \$0--\$37 under 2-corn/1-soy and zero under strict alternation, and the 90th percentile is zero under every plan in the high-productivity zones and in the northern and central medium zones. The substantive point of Section \ref{sec:results} is therefore robust to a distributional view: rotation does not just lower mean debt; it bounds the upper tail, and the gap between plans is largest in the tail that matters for lender exposure.

\subsection{\label{subsec:param_fit}Revenue dispersion under the nonparametric yield generator}
Section \ref{subsec:generator} estimates the rotation-conditional yield distribution $F_{Y \mid s, c, z}$ nonparametrically from the panel. This subsection reports the \textit{dispersion} of revenue at the rotation-plan level that the generator implies.

Figure \ref{fig:fig_revenue_cv} reports the coefficient of variation of \textbf{discounted six-year revenue} under the baseline nonparametric yield generator and the calibrated price copula. The CV ranges from 40.4\% to 53.0\%, with strict alternation lowest (40.4--47.5\%) and continuous corn highest (43.6--53.0\%). Rotation cuts the CV by 5.4--5.9 percentage points in the northern and central regions and by 2.6--3.2 points in the south, a small but stable risk-reduction in net-revenue terms, distinct from and complementary to the delinquency-probability reduction documented in Section \ref{sec:results}.

Two notes on interpretation. First, the CV here is based on \textit{discounted net} revenue, which means the price-yield covariance term has been netted out; this differs from the gross variance decomposition in Section \ref{subsec:var_decomp}, which treats the covariance term separately. Second, the simulation does not replace the nonparametric yield generator with a parametric (Beta or Gamma) alternative, so we do not report that comparison; the CV above is computed from the nonparametric residual pools throughout.

\subsection{\label{subsec:cum_cf}Cumulative cash flow under the over-limit delinquency rule}
Section \ref{subsec:survival} notes that the simulation is also run under an ``over-limit'' delinquency rule, in which an event is recorded only if the spring loan demand exceeds the credit ceiling rather than whenever the end-of-year balance is positive. The absolute delinquency rates fall under this alternative rule, naturally, since a positive balance below the credit ceiling no longer counts, but the relative ranking of plans and the sawtooth pattern in per-year hazards both survive.

Figure \ref{fig:fig_p_cf_all} reports the corresponding cumulative net cash flow over the six-year horizon. The median (solid lines) is monotonically increasing in every cell; rotation plans (orange, blue) exceed continuous corn (red) in median cumulative cash by year 6, by \$176--\$205 per acre under strict alternation and \$121--\$144 under 2-corn/1-soy in the high-productivity zones (up to \$336 and \$215 in the low zones), and the 10--90 percentile band confirms that the \textbf{lower} tail of cumulative cash flow is also higher under rotation. This is the underlying object that the over-limit delinquency rule conditions on: when the lower tail of cumulative cash is bounded away from zero, the corresponding spring loan demand stays bounded, and the over-limit event becomes rare.

\subsection{\label{subsec:abs_shapley_vals}Absolute Shapley values}
Section \ref{subsec:shapley} reports the \textit{price share} of Shapley-decomposed delinquency risk, $\phi_P / (\phi_P + \phi_Y)$, which ranges from about 84 to 102 percent across the simulation grid. The normalization conceals the underlying \textit{levels}: a 95-percent price share means very different things depending on whether total delinquency risk is 15 percentage points or 66 percentage points.

Figure \ref{fig:fig_shapley_abs} reports the absolute Shapley values $\phi_P$ and $\phi_Y$ in percentage points of delinquency probability, stacked by uncertainty source. The total bar height equals the rotation-plan-specific delinquency probability from Figure~\ref{fig:fig_p_bar_all} less the probability that arises with neither source of uncertainty active, which is under 0.7 percentage points in 21 of the 27 cell-plan combinations and reaches 9.7 points at most (southern-low under continuous corn). Continuous corn in the central-high cell comes to 32.1 percentage points, of which 31.1 are price-attributable and 1.0 yield-attributable; the tallest bar is continuous corn in northern-low at 66.3 points, and the shortest is strict alternation in southern-high at 15.0 points, almost entirely price-driven. The figure makes clear that what changes between high-risk and low-risk cells is the \textit{level} of price risk that survives the rotation's debt-reset mechanism; the yield component is small in absolute terms across the grid.

\subsection{\label{subsec:rate_sens}Interest rate and accrual window}
The baseline sets the operating-loan rate at 9 percent with nine months of accrual. Table~\ref{tab:rate_sens} reruns the 27-cell grid (correlated draws, continuous-corn initial history, the same random seeds as the baseline) at 6 and 12 percent, and the text reports the six- and twelve-month accrual windows at 9 percent. Interest enters only through the accrual factor $(1+i/12)^{m}$ applied to the spring loan, so a rate $i$ with $m$ months of accrual and a different pair with the same factor give the same results. A rate of 6 percent with nine months ($1.046$) is the same as 9 percent with six months, and 12 percent with nine months ($1.094$) is the same as 9 percent with twelve months; the simulated probabilities for these pairs agree to within 0.05 percentage points in every cell and plan (the residual reflects the very small difference between the paired accrual factors).

Moving the rate from 9 to 6 percent lowers six-year delinquency by 4.2--5.9 points under continuous corn, 3.2--5.8 under 2-corn/1-soy, and 2.5--5.4 under strict alternation; moving it to 12 percent raises it by 4.7--6.3, 3.6--5.5, and 2.7--5.2 points. The ranking of plans (continuous corn above 2-corn/1-soy above strict alternation) holds in every cell at every rate and window. The reduction from strict alternation relative to continuous corn is 10.9--26.4 points at 6 percent, 12.8--26.6 at the baseline, and 14.8--26.5 at 12 percent (cell means 17.7, 19.1, and 20.6), so the rotation effect widens modestly as credit becomes more expensive but does not depend on the 9 percent assumption.
